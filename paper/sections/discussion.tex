\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the Learned Forecast Adds}

The consistent pattern across both live campaigns is that the model's advantage
over naive persistence is concentrated in \emph{magnitude} rather than
\emph{sign}. On the Jul~31 filtered entries the two methods are
indistinguishable on directional accuracy (76.7\% vs 77.0\%), but the model
explains twice as much variance in the realized z-score (R$^2$ 0.347 vs 0.174).
The confidence filter is what selects high-$|z|$ states, and in those states the
sign of the next z-score is largely determined by persistence alone. What the
gradient-boosting model contributes is a calibrated estimate of \emph{how far}
the z-score will be from equilibrium at $t+H$.

This distinction matters for how the contribution should be read. A reader who
takes the 76.9\% directional accuracy as the headline would be measuring mostly
the filter, not the model. The defensible claim is narrower: conditional on
entering, the learned forecast ranks and sizes deviations better than
thresholding the current z-score does.

\subsection{Why Persistence Beats Mean-Reversion at One Snapshot}

Classical pairs trading enters \emph{against} the deviation, betting on
reversion. At our hold horizon that direction is unprofitable in z-units:
mechanical mean-reversion records 30.7\% directional accuracy and mean
$\text{pnl}_{\text{proxy}}$ of $-0.437$, the exact mirror of the persistence
peer on the same slots. The reason is a mismatch between the hold horizon and
the reversion half-life. One snapshot is roughly 110~seconds, while the rolling
z-score is computed over a 300-snapshot window; a spread that is two standard
deviations from its nine-hour mean is overwhelmingly likely to still be
displaced two minutes later. Reversion is a property of the window, not of the
next observation.

The implication is not that mean-reversion is wrong in cryptocurrency markets,
but that the horizon at which a signal is evaluated must match the horizon at
which the economic mechanism operates. Our $H=1$ target measures short-horizon
displacement, and the correct bet on displacement is continuation.

\subsection{Z-Score Units Capture Forecast Quality}\label{sec:proxy_gap}

The settlement proxy $\text{pnl}_{\text{proxy}} = \text{direction} \times
z_{t+H}$ measures the model's ability to correctly predict the standardized
spread level at horizon $H$. It is designed as a forecast quality metric, not as
an economic P\&L.

When the same Jul~31 trades are re-scored in basis points of realized spread
change---$\text{direction} \times \Delta S_{\text{bps}}$, the quantity a real
book would earn---the ordering between policies shifts. The learned policy has a
mean of $-0.71$~bps per trade at zero transaction cost, with a 35.5\% win rate,
while mechanical persistence is $-2.15$~bps and mechanical mean-reversion is
$+2.15$~bps (62.2\% win rate). Table~\ref{tab:bps} summarizes this difference.

This inversion between units reflects a structural property of the prediction
task: the model is trained to predict the z-score level $z_{t+H}$, which is
dominated by the slow-moving rolling mean $\hat{\mu}_w$, rather than the
instantaneous spread change $\Delta S_t$. The two targets are related but not
identical. A policy optimized for z-level forecasting need not maximize the
sign-alignment of instantaneous spread movements, and Table~\ref{tab:bps} shows
this divergence empirically.

The z-unit settlement is appropriate for evaluating forecast quality and
signal-extraction ability, which are the primary claims of this work. The
reported Sharpe ratios characterize the stability of the forecast signal rather
than an economic return stream. For production deployment, a direct spread-change
target ($\Delta S_{t,t+H}$) net of fees would align the training objective with
the economic objective, and this is a natural extension of the framework.

\subsection{Portfolio Sharpe Characterizes Forecast Stability}

The Jul~31 hourly portfolio Sharpe ratio of 2.41 is computed from only
$n=6$ hourly observations and is sensitive to the aggregation choice: 2.35 using
closed trades only, 2.41 including mark-to-market on open inventory, and 1.70
when the series is formed at snapshot rather than hourly frequency
($n=171$). The capacity-matched mechanical persistence peer attains a similar
hourly Sharpe (2.63) on the same session, confirming that both policies exploit
the same high-persistence regime in the filtered entries.

We caution against comparing this number to the 0.95--7.94 band reported in the
crypto pairs-trading literature~\cite{tadi2021evaluation, tadi2025copula}, as
those are multi-month, capital-normalized, cost-aware returns on single-exchange
different-asset pairs, whereas ours is a six-hour gross z-unit proxy on
cross-exchange same-asset spreads with a zero risk-free rate and no cost
accounting. The primary evidence for the model's advantage lies in per-trade
forecast quality (R$^2$ and directional accuracy); Sharpe is reported as a
secondary indicator of signal stability across the portfolio.

\subsection{Sensitivity of the Baseline Margin}

The 71\% improvement in mean proxy profit over mechanical persistence depends on
how scarce inventory slots are allocated. The live campaign fills slots in
encounter order, whereas the mechanical replay considers roughly 37{,}000
candidate entries and is throttled to the same cap. If both policies instead
fill slots greedily by signal strength---by $|\hat{z}|$ for the model and $|z_t|$
for the mechanical peer---the mechanical peer's mean proxy profit rises from
$+0.437$ to $+0.731$, and the model's margin narrows from 71\% to roughly 6\%
($+0.774$ vs $+0.731$). The directional-accuracy gap is far more stable under
this reallocation (77.6\% vs 69.5\%, or $+8.1$~percentage points).

We report the encounter-order numbers as the primary result because they reflect
what the live system actually did, but the ranked-capacity comparison is the
fairer test of signal quality, and readers should weight the directional-accuracy
gap more heavily than the profit ratio.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Evaluation horizon.} Each live campaign covers approximately eight hours
of wall-clock collection, of which roughly six hours carry active predictions
after the 90-snapshot z-score warmup. This is far too short to characterize
regime dependence, and the two campaigns differ in model vintage, horizon,
z-score window, and market session, so neither is a controlled ablation of the
other.

\textbf{Proxy settlement.} As detailed in Section~\ref{sec:proxy_gap}, P\&L is
measured in z-units gross of fees, slippage, and bid-ask crossing. No dollar
return is claimed.

\textbf{Execution latency.} The collector's observed cadence under full signal
load is approximately 110~seconds rather than the nominal 60~seconds, so a
signal is acted on against a market state that is up to two minutes stale.
Fischer et al.~\cite{fischer2019statistical} show that minute-scale crypto
alpha can fall from 20.5 to 3.8~bps with a one-minute delay and vanish by minute
five; we have not run the corresponding delay ablation, and it is the single
most informative robustness check still missing.

\textbf{Dependence structure.} Rows within a snapshot share the same market
state across coins and venue pairs, so the 2.9~million training rows and
59{,}000 scored predictions are not independent samples. Confidence intervals
computed under an i.i.d.\ assumption would be badly overstated, and we
deliberately report no significance tests.

\textbf{Operational artifacts.} Orderbook coverage in the 68-feature prediction
model is thinner than in the 73-feature Jul~30 variant, concentrating imbalance
signal on a subset of venues.
