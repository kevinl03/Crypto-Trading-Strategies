\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a gradient-boosting framework for predicting same-asset
cross-exchange cryptocurrency spread dynamics, replacing the mechanical z-score
threshold rules used by existing high-frequency crypto pairs-trading systems with
a learned forecast. Trained on 68 features spanning lagged spread dynamics,
orderbook imbalance, trade flow, and funding rates across 23~volatile assets and
6~centralized exchanges at minute-scale granularity, the LightGBM model predicts
the next-snapshot z-score and trades only when the predicted magnitude exceeds a
confidence threshold.

Our key findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A gradient-boosting model trained on live CEX microstructure achieves prediction R$^2$ of 0.35 and directional accuracy of 76.9\% on filtered live paper-trading entries, compared to R$^2$ of 0.17 for naive persistence on the same rows.

    \item Under matched capacity constraints, the learned policy improves per-trade
    directional accuracy by 7.6~percentage points and mean z-unit profit by 71\%
    over a mechanical persistence baseline. The directional gap is robust to how
    scarce inventory slots are allocated; the profit ratio is not, narrowing to
    roughly 6\% when both policies fill slots greedily by signal strength.

    \item The confidence filter ($|\hat{z}| \geq 0.5$) acts as a learned abstention mechanism, lifting R$^2$ by 0.19--0.25 by selecting the 10--15\% of predictions in high-signal regimes. On these filtered rows, directional accuracy is largely explained by persistence in high-$|z|$ states; the model's incremental value is in capturing the magnitude of future z-score movements.

    \item The result is a forecasting result and not a trading result. Scored in
    basis points of realized spread change rather than z-units, none of the
    policies we evaluate---including ours---has a positive mean at any non-zero
    transaction cost. Section~\ref{sec:proxy_gap} reports this reversal in full.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:limitations} sets out the threats to validity in detail; the
binding ones are the short evaluation window, the proxy settlement unit, the
approximately 110-second collector cadence, and the cross-sectional dependence
that rules out i.i.d.\ significance testing.

\subsection{Future Work}

Three directions follow naturally from this work. First, incorporating realistic fee structures and execution latency into the settlement proxy would bridge the gap between z-unit performance and economic profitability. Fischer et al.~\cite{fischer2019statistical} show that alpha in minute-scale crypto strategies can disappear within 1--5~minutes of execution delay; a systematic delay ablation would quantify this risk for our framework. Second, extending the feature set with additional non-backfillable signals (liquidation cascades, volume-tiered trading fees, sub-second quote updates) could improve forecast quality in regimes where orderbook imbalance alone is insufficient. Third, the released dataset enables the community to benchmark alternative model
architectures on the same cross-exchange microstructure panel, including sequence
models that may capture temporal dependencies the tree ensemble cannot.
