\section{Ablation Studies}\label{sec:ablation}

We conduct ablation experiments to isolate the contributions of the confidence filter, the learned forecast direction, and the microstructure feature set.

\subsection{Confidence Filter}

The $|\hat{z}| \geq 0.5$ confidence filter is a design choice that restricts trading to predictions the model is most certain about. To quantify its effect, we compare model R$^2$ on all predictions versus the filtered subset across both live campaigns.

\begin{table}[h]
\caption{Effect of the $|\hat{z}| \geq 0.5$ confidence filter on model R$^2$ across campaigns.}
\label{tab:filter_ablation}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Campaign} & \textbf{All R$^2$} & \textbf{Filtered R$^2$} & \textbf{$\Delta$} & \textbf{\% rows filtered} \\
\midrule
Jul~30 (live)  & 0.054 & 0.248 & +0.194 & 10.3\% \\
Jul~31 (live)  & 0.104 & 0.347 & +0.243 & 14.9\% \\
Offline test   & 0.133 & 0.383 & +0.250 & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The filter consistently lifts R$^2$ by approximately 0.2--0.25 across all evaluation settings, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:filter_lift}. Importantly, this lift is not free: the filtered subset constitutes only 10--15\% of all predictions, meaning 85--90\% of snapshot/pair observations are declined. The filter acts as a learned abstention mechanism, analogous to the ``skip low-magnitude predictions'' strategy shown to improve profit-per-trade in Sarmento et al.~\cite{sarmento2024ml}.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig3_filter_r2_lift.png}
\caption{Model R$^2$ before and after the $|\hat{z}| \geq 0.5$ confidence filter. The filter selects predictions in high-signal regimes, lifting R$^2$ by +0.19 to +0.25 across both live campaigns.}
\label{fig:filter_lift}
\end{figure}

A critical caveat: much of the filtered directional accuracy is explained by persistence in high-$|z|$ states rather than incremental model skill. On Jul~31 filtered rows, naive persistence achieves 77.0\% directional accuracy versus the model's 76.7\%. The model's advantage is in R$^2$ (0.347 vs 0.174), indicating that it captures the \emph{magnitude} of future z-score movements more accurately than simply predicting that large z-scores will persist.

\subsection{Learned Direction vs Mechanical Rules}

The baseline comparison in Section~\ref{sec:results} shows that the model's learned direction outperforms both mechanical alternatives on per-trade quality metrics. To further illustrate this, Figure~\ref{fig:edge} decomposes the model's directional accuracy edge over naive persistence by campaign and filter condition.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig6_model_minus_naive_diracc.png}
\caption{Directional accuracy gap: model minus naive persistence. On filtered rows, the gap is near zero (the filter selects high-persistence states where both methods succeed). The model's primary advantage is R$^2$ on magnitude, not directional hit rate on filtered entries.}
\label{fig:edge}
\end{figure}

On all predictions (unfiltered), the model's directional accuracy is \emph{lower} than naive persistence ($-$7.1~pp on Jul~30; $-$4.7~pp on Jul~31), because the model sometimes predicts reversals in states where persistence is the dominant mode. On filtered rows, the gap narrows to near zero ($+$1.1~pp on Jul~30; $-$0.3~pp on Jul~31). This pattern confirms that the confidence filter selects states where both the model and persistence agree on direction; the model's incremental value in those states is its superior fit to z-score magnitude (R$^2$), not to direction alone.

\subsection{Feature Importance: Role of Microstructure Inputs}

The model uses 68~features spanning lagged z-scores, spread dynamics, orderbook imbalance, trade flow, bid-ask spreads, cross-exchange dispersion, funding rates, and categorical identifiers. Figure~\ref{fig:importance} shows the top~20 features by LightGBM split gain.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig4_feature_importance_top20.png}
\caption{Top~20 features by LightGBM split gain. Lagged z-scores dominate, followed by spread momentum and cross-exchange dispersion. Orderbook imbalance features appear prominently in positions 15--20.}
\label{fig:importance}
\end{figure}

Three feature groups emerge:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Lagged z-scores and spread dynamics} (positions 1--3, 6, 8--10): \texttt{zscore\_lag\{1,2,3\}}, \texttt{spread\_bps\_lag\{1,2,3\}}, \texttt{zscore\_accel}, and momentum features dominate with an order-of-magnitude more split gain than other groups. This is expected: the recent z-score trajectory is the strongest single predictor of the next z-score.

    \item \textbf{Cross-exchange dispersion} (positions 12--13): \texttt{cross\_mid\_range} and \texttt{cross\_mid\_std} capture the instantaneous spread of mid-prices across venues. These features are unique to the cross-exchange setting and are not available in single-venue pairs-trading systems.

    \item \textbf{Orderbook imbalance and trade flow} (positions 11, 15, 17, 20): \texttt{ob\_imbalance\_lag\{2,3\}\_coinbase}, \texttt{tk\_spread\_bps} lags, and \texttt{tightest\_ba} contribute meaningful split gain. The L2 orderbook captures the top~20 price levels on each side, from which we derive an imbalance ratio reflecting the relative depth of bids versus asks. This ratio acts as a directional pressure signal: when ask-side volume substantially exceeds bid-side volume, the market is absorbing more selling pressure, which informs the model's prediction of where the z-score will move next. These features cannot be reconstructed from historical OHLCV or ticker data. Public exchange APIs serve historical candlestick data, but \emph{not} historical order books, quotes, or trade-level prints. Every orderbook and trade-flow observation in our dataset exists only because it was collected live, and cannot be backfilled retroactively for any past window.
\end{enumerate}

The \texttt{coin} and \texttt{pair} categoricals (positions 4--5, 9) allow the model to learn asset-specific and venue-pair-specific z-score dynamics without separate per-pair models, effectively conditioning on the structural characteristics of each cross-exchange relationship.

\subsection{Generalization: Train vs Test}

Offline train and test R$^2$ are nearly identical on the unfiltered set (0.128 vs 0.133), indicating that the model does not overfit to the training data despite a large feature space and 2,500 maximum boosting rounds. Early stopping selected iteration~74, using only 3\% of the allocated rounds. On filtered predictions, the gap widens (train 0.447 vs test 0.383), reflecting some selection of high-signal regimes that are more prevalent in training windows. The subsequent live campaigns (R$^2$ = 0.248 and 0.347 on filtered entries) confirm that the model retains predictive power on genuinely unseen market conditions collected weeks after training.
