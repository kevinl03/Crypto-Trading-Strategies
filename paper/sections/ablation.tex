\section{Ablation Studies}\label{sec:ablation}

We conduct ablation experiments to isolate the contributions of the confidence filter, the learned forecast direction, and the microstructure feature set.

\subsection{Confidence Filter}

The $|\hat{z}| \geq 0.5$ confidence filter is a design choice that restricts trading to predictions the model is most certain about. To quantify its effect, we compare model R$^2$ on all predictions versus the filtered subset across both live campaigns.

\begin{table}[h]
\caption{Effect of the $|\hat{z}| \geq 0.5$ confidence filter on model R$^2$ across campaigns.}
\label{tab:filter_ablation}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Campaign} & \textbf{All R$^2$} & \textbf{Filtered R$^2$} & \textbf{$\Delta$} & \textbf{\% rows filtered} \\
\midrule
Jul~30 (live)  & 0.054 & 0.248 & +0.194 & 10.3\% \\
Jul~31 (live)  & 0.104 & 0.347 & +0.243 & 14.9\% \\
Offline test   & 0.133 & 0.383 & +0.250 & --- \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The filter consistently lifts R$^2$ by approximately 0.2--0.25 across all evaluation settings, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:filter_lift}. Importantly, this lift is not free: the filtered subset constitutes only 10--15\% of all predictions, meaning 85--90\% of snapshot/pair observations are declined. The filter strategy parallels the ``skip low-magnitude predictions'' approach shown to improve profit-per-trade in Sarmento et al.~\cite{sarmento2024ml}.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig3_filter_r2_lift.png}
\caption{Model R$^2$ before and after the $|\hat{z}| \geq 0.5$ confidence filter. The filter selects predictions in high-signal regimes, lifting R$^2$ by +0.19 to +0.25 across both live campaigns.}
\Description{Grouped bar chart of model R squared on a vertical axis from zero to
0.35 for the two live campaigns. For Jul 30 the all-predictions bar is 0.054 and
the filtered bar is 0.248, a rise of 0.194. For Jul 31 the all-predictions bar is
0.104 and the filtered bar is 0.347, a rise of 0.243. In both campaigns the
filtered bar is roughly three times the height of the unfiltered bar.}
\label{fig:filter_lift}
\end{figure}

A critical caveat: much of the filtered directional accuracy is explained by persistence in high-$|z|$ states rather than incremental model skill. On Jul~31 filtered rows, naive persistence achieves 77.0\% directional accuracy versus the model's 76.7\%. The model's advantage is in R$^2$ (0.347 vs 0.174), indicating that it captures the \emph{magnitude} of future z-score movements more accurately than simply predicting that large z-scores will persist.

\subsection{Learned Direction vs Mechanical Rules}

The baseline comparison in Section~\ref{sec:results} shows that the model's learned direction outperforms both mechanical alternatives on per-trade quality metrics. To further illustrate this, Figure~\ref{fig:edge} decomposes the model's directional accuracy edge over naive persistence by campaign and filter condition.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig6_model_minus_naive_diracc.png}
\caption{Directional accuracy gap: model minus naive persistence. On filtered rows, the gap is near zero (the filter selects high-persistence states where both methods succeed). The model's primary advantage is R$^2$ on magnitude, not directional hit rate on filtered entries.}
\Description{Bar chart of the difference in directional accuracy between the model
and naive persistence, in percentage points, with bars above the zero line
favouring the model. The two unfiltered bars are clearly negative at about minus
7.1 percentage points for Jul 30 and minus 4.7 for Jul 31. The two filtered bars
are close to zero: slightly positive at about plus 1.1 for Jul 30 and slightly
negative at about minus 0.3 for Jul 31.}
\label{fig:edge}
\end{figure}

On all predictions (unfiltered), the model's directional accuracy is \emph{lower} than naive persistence ($-$7.1~pp on Jul~30; $-$4.7~pp on Jul~31), because the model sometimes predicts reversals in states where persistence is the dominant mode. On filtered rows, the gap narrows to near zero ($+$1.1~pp on Jul~30; $-$0.3~pp on Jul~31). This pattern confirms that the confidence filter selects states where both the model and persistence agree on direction; the model's incremental value in those states is its superior fit to z-score magnitude (R$^2$), not to direction alone.

\subsection{Feature Importance: Role of Microstructure Inputs}

The model uses 68~features spanning lagged z-scores, spread dynamics, orderbook imbalance, trade flow, bid-ask spreads, cross-exchange dispersion, funding rates, and categorical identifiers. Figure~\ref{fig:importance} shows the top~20 features by LightGBM split gain.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig4_feature_importance_top20.png}
\caption{Top~20 features by LightGBM split gain. Gain is overwhelmingly
concentrated in the lagged z-score and spread terms; microstructure features
enter only in the lower half of the ranking.}
\Description{Horizontal bar chart of the twenty highest-gain features, ordered
from largest at the top. The first bar, lagged z-score at lag one, reaches about
930,000 gain. Lagged z-score at lags three and two follow at about 400,000 and
325,000. Spread momentum, the coin categorical, lagged spread in basis points,
z-score acceleration, z-score momentum, the pair categorical, and two further
lagged spread terms range from about 115,000 down to 42,000. The remaining nine
bars are all below about 20,000 and are visually near-zero against the first bar;
they comprise per-venue bid-ask spread lags for Coinbase, Kraken and Bybit,
cross-venue mid-price range and standard deviation, two Coinbase orderbook
imbalance lags, the tightest bid-ask indicator, and a Kraken price rank.}
\label{fig:importance}
\end{figure}

Three groups emerge, but their contributions are of very different orders of
magnitude:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Lagged z-scores and spread dynamics} (ranks 1--4, 6--8, 10--11):
    \texttt{zscore\_lag\{1,2,3\}}, \texttt{spread\_bps\_lag\{1,2,3\}},
    \texttt{spread\_momentum\_1\_3}, \texttt{zscore\_momentum\_1\_3}, and
    \texttt{zscore\_accel}. These dominate completely. \texttt{zscore\_lag1}
    alone carries roughly two orders of magnitude more split gain than any
    microstructure feature.

    \item \textbf{Categorical identifiers} (ranks 5 and 9): \texttt{coin} and
    \texttt{pair} let the model learn asset-specific and venue-pair-specific
    dynamics without separate per-pair models. Notably, \texttt{coin} outranks
    every microstructure feature, indicating that a substantial part of the
    model's advantage over a single pooled autoregression is simply conditioning
    on which asset and venue pair is being traded.

    \item \textbf{Microstructure and cross-venue dispersion} (ranks 12--20):
    per-venue bid-ask spread lags (\texttt{tk\_spread\_bps\_lag3\_coinbase},
    \texttt{tk\_spread\_bps\_lag1\_kraken}, \texttt{tk\_spread\_bps\_lag3\_bybit}),
    cross-venue dispersion (\texttt{cross\_mid\_range},
    \texttt{cross\_mid\_std}), orderbook imbalance
    (\texttt{ob\_imbalance\_lag\{2,3\}\_coinbase}), and the liquidity extremes
    \texttt{tightest\_ba} and \texttt{price\_rank\_kraken}.
\end{enumerate}

We draw a deliberately conservative conclusion from this ranking. The model is,
to a first approximation, an autoregression on the recent z-score trajectory
conditioned on asset and venue-pair identity, with microstructure features
supplying a small correction. Only two of the twenty highest-gain features are
orderbook imbalance terms, both on a single venue, and no trade-flow, funding-rate,
or open-interest feature appears in the top~20 at all. The cross-venue dispersion
features (\texttt{cross\_mid\_range}, \texttt{cross\_mid\_std}) are genuinely
unavailable to single-venue systems, and the orderbook features are genuinely
non-backfillable, but neither group is doing the bulk of the predictive work
here. Establishing how much of the R$^2$ these features are responsible for
requires a leave-one-group-out retraining study that we have not run; the split-gain
ranking alone cannot settle it, since gain is confounded by the strong
correlation among the lagged z-score terms.

\subsection{Generalization: Train vs Test}

Offline train and test R$^2$ are nearly identical on the unfiltered set (0.128 vs 0.133), indicating that the model does not overfit to the training data despite a large feature space and 2,500 maximum boosting rounds. Early stopping selected iteration~74, using only 3\% of the allocated rounds. On filtered predictions, the gap widens (train 0.447 vs test 0.383), reflecting some selection of high-signal regimes that are more prevalent in training windows. The subsequent live campaigns (R$^2$ = 0.248 and 0.347 on filtered entries) confirm that the model retains predictive power on genuinely unseen market conditions collected weeks after training.
